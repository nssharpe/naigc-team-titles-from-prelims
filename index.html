Team Titles from Prelims

What changes if the team competition is decided on prelim scores and finals qualification becomes purely individual — modelled on the 2026 Nationals results, with the judging-drift question tested across three years. Short version: three of sixteen titles change hands, individual qualification spots rise about 69%, and both of the fairness objections turn out to be smaller than the round-to-round variation they would replace.

1,920 prelim athletes 1,255 finals routines · 4 sessions 16 team titles in scope 3 years of drift data

Executive summary

Decide the team competition on prelims, make finals qualification purely individual, and keep the finals sessions the same length. Applied to the 2026 results: three of sixteen team titles change hands, individual qualification opportunities rise about 69%, and the number of athletes who actually compete in a final barely moves. The two fairness objections usually raised against prelims-based team scoring both turn out to be smaller than the round-to-round variation the current system already accepts.

Finals access — close to a swap, at a higher standard

488 athletes compete in a 2026 final today; 511 would qualify. 390 keep their place, 98 lose it, 121 gain one. No current individual qualifier is displaced. The incoming group is stronger — median 25th percentile of their category×level group against 47th for those leaving — and comes from 75 clubs against 26 losing someone. Clubs with an athlete in a final rise from 95 to 109, and none lose their presence entirely.

Team results — three golds move

Of the 16 titles currently decided in finals, 3 golds and 12 of 34 medal positions change hands: WAG Level 9 (Liberty → Rhode Island), WAG Diamond (Grand Valley → Michigan) and MAG Intermediate (Virginia Tech → Vermont). All three are categories where three or more competitive teams reached the team final. Three bronzes that don't currently exist would start being awarded. Mixed-gender titles and every Silver, Open Scoring and MAG Developmental title already come from prelims and are unaffected.

Judging drift — not detectable over three years

Because the same two judges cover an apparatus for the whole competition, an athlete's prelim and finals routines are scored by the same panel — as clean a test as this data allows. Across six year×discipline cells the session trend straddles zero and flips sign by year; the day-1 vs day-2 comparison is not significant anywhere. The largest effect found in any cell is 0.72 team points, against a measured prelims-to-finals team swing of 1.59 (WAG) and 3.74 (MAG).

Open-squad scoring — costs very little

Scored against a 4-up-3-count lineup on the same prelim routines, the open squad was worth 9.35 team points in total across 45 teams — and 27 of the 45 gained nothing at all. Crucially it would not have changed a single gold; one medal position of 34 moves. It does reward deep MAG squads (r = +0.66) but not WAG ones, where four events of tightly-packed scores leave nothing to gain. Keeping the open squad — and avoiding A/B team splits — is well supported.

The bottom line. On the evidence, the fairness case against prelims-based team scoring (judge drift over prelims and large teams drawing on a bigger pool of scores) is weaker than it might be assumed to be, and the change slightly improves both the breadth and the standard of the finals field. The potential downsides are that three of sixteen titles in 2026 would have gone to a different club (unclear if this is positive, negative, or neutral), and that the team competition would no longer be settled with the contenders in the room together.

00 — Baseline

What the current system actually does

Everything below is grounded in the real 2026 files. Two facts to keep in mind:

  1. Only five level-groups have finals at all. WAG Platinum, Diamond and Level 9; MAG Advanced and Intermediate. Silver, Open Scoring and MAG Developmental have no finals session — their individual and team awards already come straight from prelims. This rule change cannot touch them.
  2. Mixed-gender team titles are already decided on prelims. Colorado State's Platinum Mixed title (112.725), UCLA's Diamond Mixed (114.875) and Ohio State's Level 9 Mixed (108.35) are all prelim numbers in the banquet program. So the proposal doesn't introduce prelims-based team scoring to NAIGC — it extends it to the remaining 16 titles.

01 — Qualifiers

Individual qualification spots would rise about 69%

Verdict

Holding each finals session to the same number of routines (which is what actually sets session length), individual qualification spots go from 663 to 1,119 — up 69% overall, and up 94% in Platinum where team qualifiers are thickest. Because the new qualifier fields are strict supersets of the old, no athlete who currently qualifies individually loses their spot.

Counting only athletes who actually perform a routine, the field grows slightly: 488 compete now, 511 would qualify. 390 keep their place, 98 lose it, 121 gain one.

The reallocation rule I used

"Same size" doesn't by itself determine how the freed slots are handed out, so I picked the assumption that best matches NAIGC's existing intent: multiply the current per-group qualification depth by a common factor, keeping ties-at-the-cutoff advancing exactly as they do today. The current depths already encode a proportional-to-participation allocation (20 for the big Collegiate Women+ groups, 2–3 for the small ones), so scaling preserves that balance rather than flattening it.

Three different things are being counted — and the overlap only matters for one of them

Nearly every all-around qualifier also wins individual apparatus spots: 183 of the 189 under the proposal, and 105 of 108 today. You cannot post a top-20 all-around score without being near the top on something. So it matters a great deal whether those athletes get counted once or several times.

Qualification spots (663 → 1,119) count each final separately, so an athlete who qualifies all-around and on vault holds two. That is deliberate — they are two finals and two sets of medals — and the current figure is counted identically, so the +69% is like-for-like.

Routine slots — the capacity that sets session length — do not double count. On each apparatus the model takes the union of the all-around qualifiers and that apparatus's own qualifiers, so an athlete who qualified both ways performs one vault, not two. That union removes 482 routines from the proposed field (1,778 down to 1,296). Had the model added them up naively it would have concluded far fewer athletes fit inside the existing session length, and badly understated the expansion.

Athlete counts (488 → 511) are the union across everything — each person once, however many finals they reach.

Who actually moves

Who the 98 who no longer qualify are

They are not the weakest athletes in the room. Team-lineup-only competitors sit at the 47th percentile of their own category×level group — a shade above its median, because teams field their strongest available on each apparatus. Only 17% are in the bottom quartile, and they averaged 2.2 routines each.

The losses are concentrated: 26 clubs account for all 98, led by Jurassic (11), Washington U. St. Louis (9), Wisconsin (7) and Florida State (7). These are the deep programs that currently convert one team berth into several finals appearances.

Who the 121 who now qualify are

Markedly stronger athletes. Their median sits at the 25th percentile of their category×level group — the top quartile — and 59% are in the top third of their group. Only 29% come from the bottom half. On the measure the sport uses to rank people, the incoming group clearly out-performs the outgoing one.

They are also spread far wider: 75 clubs gain a qualifier, against 26 clubs losing one. Only 22 of the 121 qualify in the all-around; 85 make it on a single apparatus, so between them they add 211 routines rather than a full extra field.

The net effect on breadth

Because the incoming athletes come from three times as many clubs as the outgoing ones, the number of clubs with at least one athlete competing in a final rises from 95 to 109. Fourteen clubs — Ball State, Brandeis, Rutgers, Troy, Missouri, West Virginia, Denver, Maryland and others — gain a finals presence they don't currently have, and no club loses its presence entirely. That is the clearest equity argument for the change: it converts appearances that currently concentrate in a few large squads into appearances spread across more programs, while raising the average standard of the field.

Does MAG have the numbers to support a deeper cut — or would finals become everyone?

MAG is the smaller discipline and the expansion factor is applied to already-small fields. But the numbers don't support the worry. Measured against the pool that actually competed each apparatus in prelims, no new MAG group comes close to qualifying everyone, and the two largest groups stay firmly selective.

Where the all-around rate runs highest

The one number that stands out is Advanced Collegiate Men+ all-around at 54%. But the denominator is doing the work: only 28 of the 90 athletes in that group completed all six apparatus in prelims, so the AA pool is small by nature. Qualifying 15 of 28 all-arounders is a different proposition from qualifying 15 of 90 competitors, and the six apparatus finals in that same group sit at 33–42%.

The groups that already qualify everyone are not created by this change

MAG does have a handful of 100% categories — Advanced Collegiate Women+ and Advanced Community Women+ each had a pool of one athlete in 2026. They qualify today and they qualify under the proposal. That is a consequence of how finely the categories are cut, not of how deep the qualification line is drawn, and this rule change neither causes nor worsens it. If those categories feel unsatisfying, the lever is category structure, not cut depth.

Who actually leaves the MAG final: 19 athletes, 6 clubs

Every one of them currently gets in through a team lineup rather than their own score. They sit at a median of 14th in a group of about 27, and the concentration is striking — Jurassic (6), Wisconsin (4), Vermont (3), Washington U. St. Louis (3), 5280 (2) and Arizona State (1) account for all 19. Five of the nineteen competed a single routine in the whole final; the lowest-placed was ranked 85th of 90 in Advanced Collegiate Men+ and performed once.

Who arrives: 34 athletes, 22 clubs

This is the part that answers the "prelims feel silly" concern most directly, because the incoming group is defined by prelim performance in a way the outgoing group is not:

  • 16 of the 34 are top-20 all-arounders in their own group who currently have no route into the final at all — Texas A&M at 68.05 (12th of 90), Flippin' Illini at 67.90 (13th), UCLA at 67.85 (15th). Their clubs did not qualify a team, and they missed the all-around cut by a place or two.
  • 10 are genuine event specialists — outside the top 40 all-around but inside the top 20 on one apparatus. A UMass Amherst gymnast ranked 54th all-around but 9th on high bar; a UCLA gymnast 69th all-around but 12th on floor. Under the present rules a specialist at a club without a team is invisible.
  • Only 4 of the 34 qualify all-around; 24 make it on a single apparatus. They enter fairly evenly across the six events — vault 8, floor 7, pommel 6, high bar 5, rings 5, parallel bars 3 — so no single apparatus is carrying the expansion.
  • They come from 22 clubs against the 6 losing places, led by Texas A&M (4) and Flippin' Illini (3).

In Summary

MAG's final would go from 127 competing athletes to 142 — 19 out, 34 in. Prelims arguably matter more, not less: today a mid-pack athlete at a club with a team reaches the final while a 12th-place all-arounder or a 9th-place high bar worker at a club without one does not. The deeper cut is what lets prelim performance, rather than club affiliation, decide who competes. And at 19–54% depending on group, the line is still a line.

02 — Team results

Three of sixteen titles change hands

Verdict

Of the 16 team titles currently decided in finals, 3 golds move (19%) and 12 of 34 medal positions (35%) end up with a different club. Three bronze medals that don't currently exist would start being awarded, because several categories had fewer than three teams reach the team final.

The prelim and finals team scores are not far apart on average — WAG teams score +0.71 higher in finals (SD 1.59), MAG teams score −4.21 lower (SD 3.74) — but the margins at the top are tiny, so small differences flip places.

Why the flips happen where they do

All three golds that move come from categories where three or more genuinely competitive teams reached the team final and finished within about two points of each other. In the eight categories where only one team qualified, and in MAG Advanced Collegiate Men+ where the top three were separated by 5.7 points, the two rounds agree completely. The change doesn't make team results unstable in general — it makes them different in exactly the races that were already close.

03 — Judging drift

Across three years, session drift is not detectable — and would be smaller than the swing it replaces

Verdict

Tested over six year×discipline cells (2024, 2025, 2026 × WAG, MAG), the linear session trend straddles zero and flips sign by year. Pooled across three years it is −0.011 points per routine per session step in WAG (p = 0.17) and −0.013 in MAG (p = 0.47) — both indistinguishable from zero.

Testing day 1 against day 2 directly gives the same answer: pooled −0.53 team points in WAG (p = 0.15) and −0.48 in MAG (p = 0.71), with 2025 running the other way. Only one of six cells (2026 WAG) shows even marginal session-to-session variation, implying a spread of about 0.72 team points.

For comparison, the same teams' scores move by 1.59 team points (WAG) and 3.74 (MAG) between prelims and finals. The noise you would be adding is smaller than the noise you would be removing — and unlike the drift, that round-to-round swing is measured, not hypothesised.

Why this test is clean

The judging structure makes this comparison unusually well-controlled. Each gym runs one level group and only that group — Green = Platinum, Red = Diamond, Yellow = Level 9 / Silver / Open, Orange = all MAG. And within that, the same two judges cover a given apparatus for the entire competition: every Silver bars routine at Nationals 2026 was scored by the same pair of judges, in every session, on both days, and again in finals. In WAG a panel is fixed to one apparatus and one level; in MAG a panel covers one apparatus across all three levels.

The consequence is that panel identity is constant by construction. When an athlete's prelim score is compared with their finals score on the same apparatus, the same two people scored both routines. Nothing about who was judging can explain a difference — so anything the test picks up is genuine within-panel movement over the weekend: fatigue, recalibration, anchoring on what they have already seen. That is exactly the effect the rule change would expose teams to, and it is what the numbers above measure.

Clubs almost never split across sessions (299 of 306 club×level blocks sit in a single session), which is why a session effect would translate directly into a team advantage — and why every test here clusters its errors by club.

Day 1 vs. day 2

Sessions 1–3 run on day 1 and 4–6 on day 2, so the day split can be tested directly rather than inferred from a trend line. It is the sharper version of the fairness question: if a panel warms up or tires overnight, teams competing on different days face different standards.

One apparent effect that dissolves on inspection

A first pass flagged 2024 MAG as showing large session-to-session variation (p = 0.066, an implied spread of 5.3 team points). It comes entirely from session 2, which contributed exactly one all-around finalist — six routines from a single athlete, who dropped 5.15 in the all-around between rounds on the back of a −2.65 pommel horse and a −1.90 parallel bars. Three other session-2 finalists competed only partial lineups and so fall outside this sample. An n-of-1 is not a session effect. Dropping sessions represented by fewer than three clubs, 2024 MAG's p-value goes to 0.60 and its estimated spread to zero, leaving 2026 WAG (p = 0.075, 0.72 team points) as the only cell of six with anything in it.

The comparison that matters

04 — Open squad

Open-squad scoring costs little, and only part of it is about squad size

Verdict

Prelims scores the top 3 of everyone who competed; finals is 4 up, 3 count from a lineup picked per apparatus. Comparing like with like — the same prelim routines scored under a 4-up-3-count lineup versus the open squad — the open-squad rule was worth 9.35 team points in total across all 45 teams. The median team gained nothing: 27 of 45 got no benefit whatsoever. Means are 0.18 (WAG) and 0.29 (MAG); the largest single gain was 1.53, and only three teams cleared a full point.

Most importantly, switching to a declared four would not have changed a single gold. All 16 titles are identical either way, and just one medal position of 34 moves — a silver/bronze swap in WAG Diamond Collegiate Women+.

Where it does bite is MAG, and there it tracks squad size as you would expect (r = +0.66, p = 0.021). WAG shows no relationship at all (r = −0.02). Six events and wider score dispersion give a deep MAG squad something to gain; four events of tightly-packed Xcel scores give a deep WAG squad almost nothing.

How this is measured

For every team that reached a team final, and for each apparatus separately, the comparison scores the same prelim routines two ways:

  • Open squad — the best three prelim scores from everyone in the squad who competed that apparatus. This is the current rule, and what the proposal would use.
  • 4-up-3-count — the best three from a four-athlete lineup. The four are the athletes the club actually fielded on that apparatus in the final (in WAG, those marked Included); where the club fielded only three, the fourth slot is backfilled from the rest of the squad using the best prelim score available on that apparatus.

The backfill matters: without it, a gymnast missing an apparatus on finals day would show up as a scoring advantage that has nothing to do with squad depth. Filling the slot holds both sides at four and leaves depth as the only difference. It was needed in 14 of 204 team×apparatus cells.

Why a four-athlete team nets to zero — the Jurassic case

With exactly four gymnasts the squad is the lineup, so the two totals must agree — and under this comparison they do. It is worth walking through, because it is what the backfill is for.

Jurassic Gymnastics in MAG Advanced Community Men+ fielded four. All four competed pommel horse in prelims — 10.75 / 10.65 / 9.80 / 9.25 — giving an open-squad top three of 31.20. In the final, Boaz Floor did not compete pommel, so only three went up. Taking just those three would drop his 10.65 and leave 29.80, an apparent 1.40-point "advantage" for the open squad. But that gap is a finals-day absence, not depth. Backfilling the vacant fourth slot with Floor's own prelim mark restores the lineup to 10.75 / 10.65 / 9.80 / 9.25 and the two totals match exactly. Jurassic's gain across all six apparatus is 0.00, as it should be.

Does it change any result?

The one caveat on this measurement

The finals lineup was chosen after prelims, so coaches already knew who had hit. That makes it a better-than-fair proxy for what a team would have declared in advance, and therefore makes the measured advantage a mild under-estimate of what a true declared-lineup rule would cost. It can also only be computed for the 45 teams that reached a team final; the smaller teams that would newly be ranked under this proposal have no finals lineup to compare against.

05 — Also worth weighing

Four other consequences I'd want on the table

You lose the head-to-head moment

Panels and equipment are already common to both rounds, so the difference isn't the judging conditions — it's that the team final is the one time the contenders are in the room together, at the same time, with the title live. Prelims-based scoring means Michigan and GVSU settle a Diamond title in separate sessions, possibly on separate days, without either knowing what it needs. That's a real cost even though — as Section 03 shows — it's a smaller statistical cost than people assume. It's the part that's hardest to quantify and easiest to feel.

It makes NAIGC more internally consistent

Right now, mixed-gender team titles and every team title in Silver, Open Scoring and MAG Developmental already come from prelims, while the other 16 come from finals. Two different rules produce two different kinds of team champion. Unifying on prelims removes an inconsistency most competitors probably can't articulate but do notice.

Finals changes character

Not in volume — slightly more athletes compete (488 → 511) and each does about the same amount (2.57 → 2.54 routines on average). What changes is the shape of the field: it polarises. Full all-arounders rise from 141 to 189 and single-apparatus specialists from 178 to 227, while the middle empties out — athletes competing a partial lineup fall from 169 to 95.

That middle is exactly what team qualification produces: a gymnast up on two or three events because that is where the team needed them. Remove it and a final becomes a room of all-arounders and event specialists, with much less in between. Whether that is the product you want is a values question, not a data question, but it should be named rather than backed into.

The shape of the weekend changes

Team titles would be settled the moment the last prelim session ends. Every club knows its team result before finals day, and the team awards could be presented a day earlier rather than anchoring the banquet. That cuts both ways: teams out of contention lose their reason to peak on day three, but teams that won get to celebrate with their whole squad present rather than the four who happened to be in the lineup. It also means finals becomes unambiguously an individual championship, which is easier to explain to newcomers than the current hybrid.

Two rules that are worth consideration

Neither of these blocks the change — both already exist in some form today — but a deeper individual cut and a wider team ranking would each make them more visible, so they are worth a look while the rule is open.

How deep vault fields should go. Xcel vault scores are coarse, so ties at the cutoff already inflate vault fields: Platinum Collegiate Women+ currently has 33 vault qualifiers against a nominal depth of 20. Deepening the cut compounds that — the model puts 61 athletes in that vault final. If that feels like too many, a cap or a tie-break on vault specifically would keep the field closer to the intended depth.

How team ties should resolve. Prelim team scores tie readily — Platinum Collegiate Women+ already had a three-way tie at 113.225 for the last qualifying spot. Ranking 43 teams rather than 9 makes ties on the podium more likely than they are now, so it may be worth settling in advance whether the answer is highest single event, best drop score, or simply co-champions.
